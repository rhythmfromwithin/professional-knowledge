# Remembering Is Not Enough: Evaluating Timing and Omission in Longitudinal AI Mental Health Support
> 原文: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08721](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08721)

arXiv:2611.08721v1 Announce Type: new
Abstract: Evaluations of large language models (LLMs) for mental health support commonly focus on the clinical appropriateness of individual responses, potentially overlooking failures that emerge across extended interactions. As AI assistants accumulate memory of users' circumstances and prior disclosures, this raises an accountability question: Do AI chatbots provide appropriate support as users' needs evolve? We investigate by analyzing 14,256 longitudinal simulations grounded in donated chatbot histories from 22 users, examining how a memory-equipped assistant responds to suicidal ideation, self-harm, and anxiety crises across recovery trajectories and communication styles. We introduce the Context-Aware and Readiness-Evaluative (CARE) framework, developed with mental health professionals, to assess the timing and quality of advice, including omissions. Advice was less aligned with users' readiness after a crisis than in matched control stretches of the same conversation, largely because of premature recommendations, which were most common when recovery stalled. Revisiting a crisis reduced premature advice but not lagging advice, and users' communication style changed how often and when the assistant gave recommendations. Although explicitly unproductive advice was rare, the assistant frequently omitted clinician-expected support such as follow-up and planning for next steps. These findings show the limits of single response-level safety evaluation and the need to assess timing, omission, and consistency across users when holding AI systems accountable for longitudinal support.
