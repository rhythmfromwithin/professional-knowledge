# Who Gets Blamed for the Blackout? A Critical Incident Analysis of Clients, Reputation Systems, and Online Freelancers
> 原文: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.28365](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.28365)

arXiv:2611.28365v1 Announce Type: new
Abstract: Online labor platforms such as Fiverr and Upwork judge freelancers by how fast they reply, whether they deliver on time, and what clients say about them. In the Global South, these signals depend on electricity and internet connections that workers do not control. This paper asks how infrastructure failures, such as power outages, internet failures, and government internet shutdowns, become lasting reputational penalties in the gig economy, and how freelancers prevent or repair them. We use the critical incident technique to analyze 149 incidents from 30 interviews with online freelancers in Bangladesh, including accounts of the July 2024 nationwide internet shutdown. Most breakdowns were absorbed without lasting harm. Of the 21 that became lasting penalties, 17 passed through clients. A client who could not see the power cut or the shutdown read the worker's silence as unreliability and left, canceled, or gave a bad rating. Platform reputation systems then stored that judgment without its context. We call this two-stage process client-mediated misattribution. Freelancers responded with four repair strategies: making the context visible, making the breakdown invisible, staying visible to the platform, and reducing dependence on any single platform or client. Exposure and the capacity to repair were unequal, and urban participants were the most exposed. The study extends research on algorithmic management, attribution in distributed work, and information and communication technologies for development (ICT4D), and it proposes outage-aware design for platform rating and reputation systems.
