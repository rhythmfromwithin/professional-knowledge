# AI, Specialization, and the Organization of Knowledge Work: Evidence from Legal Services
> 原文: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23354](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23354)

arXiv:2611.23354v1 Announce Type: new
Abstract: Artificial intelligence makes specialized knowledge cheaper to use, but its effects on professional-service firms depend on how clients reallocate work and how suppliers respond. We develop a framework linking individual self-provision, enterprise make-or-buy decisions, and pricing by specialist firms. As AI improves lower-priced alternatives, clients may reallocate work across outside providers without bringing it in-house. If the departing work is more price-sensitive than the work that remains, premium firms may raise their optimal markups even as their share of work declines. We examine these margins in U.S. legal services. Across 89 federal districts, a difference-in-differences analysis finds a 6.6-percentage-point increase in non-prisoner self-representation relative to prisoner petitions by fiscal 2025, remaining substantial after adjusting for pre-existing trends. In contrast, lawyer employment shows no clear post-2022 shift toward client firms. Legal-AI benchmarks reveal continuing limitations in reliably completing complex, multi-step work, offering one possible explanation. Meanwhile, the largest law firms reported 13% revenue growth in 2025 despite nearly flat billable hours, while lower-priced firms experienced stronger demand growth. Reported AI integration is also stronger among firms with lower revenue per lawyer. These patterns highlight the importance of changes in work allocation and supplier competition, even as much of the underlying reallocation predates generative AI. The findings illustrate why cheaper expertise need not produce either widespread internalization or declining prices for premium professional services.
